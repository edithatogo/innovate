A modeled “what if” comparison describes outcomes under specified model
assumptions. Estimating a causal effect from observed outcomes additionally
requires a design and identification assumptions. Use the
[scenario tutorial](/innovate/tutorials/scenario-experiment-workflows/) for the
first task. This tutorial examines a small native cross-fitted contrast and
makes its calculation and limits explicit.

## Learning outcomes

You will reproduce six held-out nuisance predictions and AIPW scores, understand
why the estimate differs from a simple difference in sample means, and inspect
training-arm overlap. You will also verify that insufficient arm counts,
insufficient distinct clusters and clusters split across folds are rejected.

## Prerequisites

Use the [native development environment](/innovate/user-guide/installation/) and
basic familiarity with treatment indicators and averages. The six observations
are deliberately synthetic. They are not clinical or policy data and do not
identify an empirical treatment effect.

The operation is `summarize_causal` with method `cross_fitted_stratum_ate`.
All observations share one stratum. Fold one contains observations 1–3; fold two
contains observations 4–6. Nuisance values for each held-out fold are estimated
from the other fold. Cross-fitting and orthogonal scores have a broader
statistical theory described by
[Chernozhukov and colleagues (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1111/ectj.12097).
This fixture checks a particular stratum-based calculation; it does not implement
or validate every estimator discussed in that work.

## Interpretation

For observation `i`, the checked score is
`mu1 - mu0 + T * (Y - mu1) / e - (1 - T) * (Y - mu0) / (1 - e)`.
Here `e` is the training fold's treatment fraction and `mu0`, `mu1` are its
control and treated outcome means within the stratum.

| Held-out observation | Outcome | Treatment | Training propensity | Training control mean | Training treated mean | Score |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 1 | 0 | 2/3 | 4 | 5.5 | 10.5 |
| 2 | 2 | 0 | 2/3 | 4 | 5.5 | 7.5 |
| 3 | 3 | 1 | 2/3 | 4 | 5.5 | -2.25 |
| 4 | 4 | 0 | 1/3 | 1.5 | 3 | -2.25 |
| 5 | 5 | 1 | 1/3 | 1.5 | 3 | 7.5 |
| 6 | 6 | 1 | 1/3 | 1.5 | 3 | 10.5 |

The score average is 5.25. For this unclustered calculation, the example also
checks the influence standard error against the score-based formula
`sqrt(sum((score - mean_score)^2) / (n * (n - 1)))`. These calculations validate
the implemented arithmetic, not the coverage of an interval with six observations.

**Reader task:** recover the first score without calling the library. Its outcome
regression difference is 1.5. The control residual correction is
`-(1 - 4) / (1 - 2/3) = 9`, giving 10.5. The large correction illustrates why
small training samples and limited overlap deserve scrutiny even when a
threshold passes.

## Limitations

A causal interpretation requires assumptions including consistency, appropriate
handling of interference, exchangeability given the chosen covariates, and
positivity. [Cole and Hernán (2008)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2732954/)
explain the substantive consistency, exchangeability and positivity requirements.
Passing an overlap-count threshold cannot establish them. The
stratum-based nuisance estimates here also do not constitute a general-purpose
machine-learning pipeline or resolve unmeasured confounding.

Cluster identifiers must represent the actual dependence structure. Repeated
rows from one cluster do not create additional independent training clusters;
the example checks that distinction and rejects a cluster spanning folds.
Four synthetic clusters do not establish reliable cluster-based inference.

For a model-based intervention comparison, return to
[scenario experiments](/innovate/tutorials/scenario-experiment-workflows/).
For empirical analysis, define the estimand and design before interpreting this
operation's output. The [native guide](/innovate/native-migration/) records
remaining scientific acceptance requirements.
